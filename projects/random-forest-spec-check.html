Research

A permutation-based random forest diagnostic for linear mixed models

Independent research · Sole author · Department of Statistics, University of Washington · 2025

A linear mixed model can be wrong in several ways at once, and the standard diagnostics are not well suited to finding out which. RandomForestSpecCheck takes a different route: it asks a random forest to predict the fitted model's residuals from the covariates, and uses a permutation test that respects the clustered design to decide whether the forest's success is real. The result is a single verdict on whether the model has left systematic structure behind, without any commitment in advance to the form that structure might take.

The problem with checking a mixed model

Linear mixed models are the standard instrument for correlated data: repeated measures on a subject, students inside classrooms, patients inside clinics. They model the correlation rather than ignoring it, and the price is that several assumptions must hold at once, covering the functional form of the fixed effects, the structure of the random effects, and the residual covariance. A failure in any one can bias estimates or, more insidiously, leave them intact while corrupting the standard errors.

The conventional checks each have a characteristic blind spot. A residual plot is a judgement call, and a dense cloud of several thousand points absorbs a good deal of structure before it looks anything other than round. Information criteria compare only the models an analyst thought to fit. Formal tests are aimed at one alternative at a time. All of them return the analyst to the same difficulty: you have to guess what is wrong before you can test for it.

If a mixed model is correctly specified, its residuals carry no information about the covariates. RandomForestSpecCheck hands the residuals and the covariates to a random forest and asks it to find some anyway. A permutation test then decides whether what the forest found could plausibly have arisen from noise.

The method

Fit the mixed model, extract the conditional residuals, and regress them on the predictors an analyst suspects were mishandled — time, the cluster identifier, a covariate that entered only linearly — using a random forest. The measure of the forest's success is its out-of-bag coefficient of determination,

R²oob = 1 − MSEoob / var(êij)

where each tree is scored only on the observations its bootstrap sample left out. That gives a nearly unbiased estimate of how well the forest generalises, which matters more here than in an ordinary prediction problem: a forest allowed to score itself on the data it was grown from would fit residual noise perfectly, and the diagnostic would flag every model ever fitted.

Under correct specification the conditional expectation of a residual given any predictor is zero, so the best achievable out-of-bag R² is zero up to sampling variability. The null and alternative are

H0: E[e | X] = 0   versus   H1: E[e | X] ≠ 0

tested without specifying any parametric alternative: the analyst supplies the variables, not the functional form.

The statistic has no tractable sampling distribution, so the null is simulated by permutation — within clusters, because shuffling freely would destroy the correlation structure along with the signal. Under the null, residuals are exchangeable inside a cluster, so permuting them severs the link to the predictors while leaving the joint distribution alone. Refitting the forest B times gives a null under a design identical to the real one, valid in finite samples with no appeal to asymptotics and no assumption of normality.

Large values indicate leftover structure, so the test is one-sided against the 97.5th percentile. Significance alone is a weak standard, though: with enough data, structure explaining a fraction of a percent of residual variance clears any percentile. The procedure therefore flags a model only when both criteria are met,

R²oob > Q0.975   and   R²oob > τ

with τ = 0.05 by default, so that leftover structure must account for at least five percent of residual variance before it is worth an analyst's attention. The default is a judgement rather than a derivation, and is exposed as an argument for that reason.

What the method detects, and what it does not

Four simulation studies were run, each generating data under a specific violation and fitting a model that omitted it. Three concern the mean structure: an omitted quadratic fixed effect, an omitted random slope, and unmodelled AR(1) correlation. The fourth concerns the variance: residual variance increasing with time, fitted as homoscedastic. Every cell was replicated one hundred times.

The three mean-structure studies behave as a diagnostic should. False positives are held between one and three percent against a nominal five, and power depends on the size of the omitted effect and the signal-to-noise ratio of the design: a strong quadratic trend is caught in eighty to one hundred percent of replicates, a weak one rarely. Omitted random slopes show the same pattern with an added dependence on cluster size. Unmodelled autocorrelation is the hardest, reaching about a quarter of replicates at ρ = 0.6.

The fourth study is the one worth dwelling on. Heteroscedastic residuals were essentially never flagged, in any design. This is not a failure of the implementation but a consequence of what the statistic measures: the forest predicts the residual, not its magnitude, and where the variance grows while the mean stays at zero there is no conditional mean to recover. From the point of view of the statistic the model is adequate, and the statistic says so.

Scope

RandomForestSpecCheck is a test for unmodelled mean structure, not a general test of model adequacy. Pure variance misspecification, heavy tails, and departures from normality that leave the conditional mean at zero will not be flagged and should not be expected to be. Where those are the concern, variance functions and residual-spread diagnostics remain the right instruments, and this procedure is a complement to them rather than a replacement.

Application: the Framingham Heart Study

The diagnostic was applied to a subset of the Framingham Heart Study: more than 2,600 cholesterol measurements at two-year intervals over ten years, with baseline age, sex, and body mass index. Five models were fitted — four specifications a careful analyst might reasonably choose between, and a fifth that omits time from the fixed effects altogether, a deliberate act of sabotage against a variable known on biological grounds to matter.

The intended result holds: the four reasonable specifications were cleared and the sabotaged model flagged, at an observed R² of 0.191 against a threshold of 0.05. Residual-versus-fitted plots for all five produce dense clouds centred at zero, and the sabotaged model's is not visibly different. The misspecification was in the mean structure, where the forest was looking and the eye was not.

The second reading is less comfortable. All five models, including the four that passed, have an observed R² above their own permutation 97.5th percentile; that criterion separated nothing here. What separated the sabotaged model was the practical threshold. Because permuted residuals contain no signal, the null R² is reliably negative, its upper percentile sits below zero, and almost any real fit clears it. That is what the second criterion was introduced for, and it sharpens a limitation: on data of this scale the operating characteristics are governed by τ, and an analyst accepting the default without thinking about the effect size it encodes would be delegating the decision to a number they had not chosen.

Limitations

The procedure detects mean structure and only mean structure. Power against subtle effects is modest and depends jointly on the number of clusters, the observations within them, and the noise level, so a null result in a small or noisy study is weak evidence of adequacy. The forest can only find structure in variables it is given. And because the permutation preserves clusters, a wholly omitted cluster-level effect will not be flagged; that failure mode is caught by simpler means, but it has to be caught by something. The method is a screening instrument, most useful as the first question asked rather than the last.

The function

The procedure is packaged as a single R function over ranger and dplyr. It takes a fitted model, the data, a formula naming the predictors to search, the number of permutations, and the threshold, and prints a verdict alongside the numbers behind it.

RandomForestSpecCheck(
  fitted_model = my_lmm,
  data         = my_data,
  formula_rf   = resid_model ~ time + cluster,
  B            = 500,
  threshold    = 0.05
)
RandomForestSpecCheck: Random Forest Model Specification Diagnostic for LMMs

Practical threshold:                        0.05
Observed R-squared:                         0.191
Permutation mean R-squared and 95% CI:     -0.009 (-0.011, -0.006)

Result: Model likely misspecified

Interpretation: The observed R-squared exceeds both the
upper bound of the null distribution and the practical
threshold, indicating that the model leaves systematic
structure in the residuals. This suggests that the model
may be misspecified.

The output is deliberately verbose. A diagnostic that returns a bare logical invites an analyst to treat it as a verdict, and it is not one; it is a signal that some named variables are still doing work the model has not accounted for. The next step is to read the forest's variable-importance measures, attempt the corresponding parametric extension, and check whether the flag clears.

Contribution

Permutation inference and tree ensembles are both long established, and residual analysis with machine learning has been explored for ordinary and generalised linear models. What had not been assembled was the combination in a mixed-model setting: a within-cluster permutation scheme that respects the nested design, a forest-derived statistic with an interpretable scale, a decision rule that separates significance from magnitude, and a simulation programme mapping where the procedure has power and where it does not, including an explicit negative result. The work was written for submission to Computational Statistics & Data Analysis.
